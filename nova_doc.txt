
NOVA: An Embodied Conversational Agent for Mental Health Research Using Multimodal Signal Fusion and Adaptive Questioning




Abstract—Existing conversational mental-health agents such as Woebot [1] and Tess [2] rely mainly on text-based, rule-based dialogue, while embodied research systems such as SimSensei/Ellie [3] fuse only a limited set of perceptual channels for semi-structured interviewing. Companionship-oriented agents such as Replika [4] and appearance-focused studies [5] address engagement and trust rather than assessment. Recent systematic reviews [6],[7] and an ethical survey of chatbots [8] identify multimodal fusion and adaptive dialogue as open research directions, and reinforcement-learning-based style adaptation [9] demonstrates the feasibility of learning-driven conversational behaviour. This paper presents NOVA, an embodied conversational research agent for mental-health-related signal assessment that (i) fuses facial, ocular, vocal, linguistic and optional physiological biomarkers through a cross-modal Transformer, (ii) uses reinforcement learning to adaptively select the next question from a fixed, reviewed question bank rather than adapting communication style alone, (iii) is delivered through a Unity-based embodied virtual human, and (iv) logs timestamped multimodal evidence in a researcher-facing longitudinal dashboard. We describe the system architecture, methodology, and staged evaluation plan intended for the DAIC-WOZ/E-DAIC benchmark [10]–[12], and compare NOVA against nine existing systems and reviews [1]–[9]. NOVA is positioned as a research-assessment platform rather than a diagnostic or clinical tool.
Keywords—multimodal fusion; cross-modal Transformer; reinforcement learning; adaptive questioning; embodied conversational agent; mental-health research; affective computing; DAIC-WOZ.
I. Introduction
Mental-health conditions are widely under-recognised because conventional assessment depends on brief, self-reported interviews and a clinician's subjective interpretation, motivating a growing body of conversational-agent research aimed at supporting — not replacing — this process. Text-based agents such as Woebot [1] deliver cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) content and mood check-ins validated through a randomised controlled trial, while Tess [2] combines emotion-recognition algorithms with scripted interventions and crisis alerts for scalable emotional support. Both remain limited to text and do not sense the participant directly.
Embodied systems extend this line of work: SimSensei/Ellie [3] is a semi-structured virtual-human interviewer that perceives facial expressions, gaze, voice tone and non-verbal behaviour to support objective distress assessment, and remains the closest prior system to the one proposed here. In parallel, companionship-oriented agents such as Replika [4] emphasise self-disclosure and trust rather than assessment, and experimental work on virtual-human appearance [5] shows that avatar likability and trust materially affect user engagement, informing embodied-agent design choices more broadly. Two systematic reviews [6],[7] synthesise evidence for AI conversational agents and explicitly identify multimodal fusion and personalisation as an important but under-explored direction, while a review of AI chatbots in mental health [8] surveys technical and ethical challenges, including the need for safety constraints around CBT, DBT and motivational-interviewing content. Separately, Tamantini et al. [9] show that reinforcement learning (Thompson Sampling) combined with prompted language generation can adaptively modulate a conversational agent's communication style based on multimodal emotion recognition — demonstrating that learning-driven adaptation is feasible in this space, even though its target (style) differs from assessment-oriented question selection.
Three gaps emerge from [1]–[9]: (1) no system combines facial, ocular, vocal, linguistic and physiological signals through a unified cross-modal fusion architecture; (2) reinforcement learning has been used for communication style [9] but not for adaptive assessment-question selection; and (3) no system pairs perceptual sensing with a researcher-facing environment that timestamps evidence and supports longitudinal comparison across sessions. This paper addresses these gaps with NOVA, an embodied research platform that fuses multimodal biomarkers with a cross-modal Transformer, selects its next question with a reinforcement-learning policy restricted to a fixed, reviewed question bank, and logs every turn for a longitudinal research dashboard, benchmarked against the DAIC-WOZ/E-DAIC corpus [10]–[12].
The remainder of this paper is organised as follows. Section II surveys related systems [1]–[9]. Section III presents NOVA's architecture. Section IV compares NOVA against prior systems across three tables. Section V details the methodology and reinforcement-learning formulation. Section VI outlines implementation and evaluation. Section VII discusses limitations, and Section VIII concludes.

III. Proposed System Architecture
NOVA is organised as a layered pipeline (Fig. 1). A participant interacts naturally with a Unity-rendered embodied virtual human, speaking and looking at the avatar with no typed input required. The Unity front end streams camera and microphone data to a Python backend, which runs speech-to-text, voice/acoustic analysis, face analysis, eye/gaze analysis, and, only where separately validated, camera-based remote photoplethysmography (rPPG). Each modality is converted into a compatible encoded representation and fused through a cross-modal Transformer, whose output feeds (a) an assessment-oriented state, (b) a fused multimodal state for reinforcement learning, and (c) timestamped evidence for the research dashboard. A reinforcement-learning policy then selects the next question from a fixed, reviewed question bank — the core adaptive-questioning innovation of this work — and the cycle repeats. The Python backend orchestrates this loop through an HTTP/WebSocket API, while the dashboard consumes session logs for timelines, modality traces, and longitudinal comparison; it is explicitly a research dashboard rather than a clinical one.

V. Methodology and Reinforcement Learning
Table III lists the technique used in each layer of NOVA. Speech is transcribed with a pretrained ASR model (e.g., Whisper) into a timestamped transcript, from which text embeddings (e.g., BERT-family encoders) are derived; acoustic features (MFCCs, pitch, jitter, shimmer, HNR, optionally Wav2Vec 2.0) characterise vocal delivery; validated pipelines (e.g., OpenFace, MediaPipe) extract facial action units and gaze/blink/pupil features; and rPPG-based heart-rate/HRV estimation is included only after its signal quality is validated on sample recordings. Each modality first passes through a unimodal baseline before alignment and fusion via a cross-modal Transformer, with an explicit missing-modality masking policy so the system degrades gracefully rather than fabricating values.
The reinforcement-learning module — inspired by, but redefined from, the style-adaptation framework of Tamantini et al. [9] — is restricted to selecting one predefined question from a reviewed bank; it never generates unconstrained clinical questions. The state is the fused multimodal representation plus dialogue history and topic coverage; the action is question selection; and the reward combines information gain, topic coverage and engagement, penalised for repetition, irrelevance and safety violations:
R = w1·InfoGain + w2·TopicCoverage + w3·Engagement − w4·Repetition − w5·Irrelevance − w6·SafetyViolation   (1)
The policy (e.g., PPO or Thompson Sampling) is trained per episode (one complete session). The intended benchmark is the DAIC-WOZ/E-DAIC corpus [10]–[12], accessed officially and split at the participant level to prevent leakage; because the common AVEC-2017 DAIC-WOZ task uses PHQ-8-based labels, the exact target, task type and split must be documented before any classifier claim is made. Primary metrics are AUROC and F1 (plus precision, recall, accuracy); the RL policy is separately evaluated on information gain, topic coverage, turns-to-coverage, engagement, cumulative reward and safety-violation rate, since AUROC/F1 do not characterise a question-selection policy.
VI. Implementation and Evaluation Plan
NOVA is built as a staged pipeline rather than a single monolithic system: dataset audit and label definition; participant-level splitting; unimodal baselines for voice, text, face, gaze and (if validated) rPPG; fusion ablation (Face; +Voice; +Gaze; +Text; +rPPG only if quality checks pass); early- vs. late- vs. cross-modal-Transformer fusion comparison; RL environment, question bank and training against a fixed-question baseline; the research dashboard; a FastAPI backend; and Unity integration, following an incremental ladder — dummy calls, live speech-to-text, single-modality inference, fused inference, and finally RL-selected questioning with full session logging — so integration defects are isolated one layer at a time.
Evaluation proceeds in three tiers: an offline benchmark on a locked held-out DAIC-WOZ/E-DAIC [10]–[12] partition reporting AUROC/F1/precision/recall/accuracy; an RL simulation comparing the adaptive policy against a fixed-question baseline across seeds on information gain, coverage, engagement, reward and safety; and, only after institutional ethics approval and informed consent, a small human pilot using pseudonymous identifiers and aggregate reporting, which does not support diagnostic claims. 

VII. Discussion and Limitations
NOVA is framed strictly as a research-assessment platform: gaze avoidance, vocal instability or an rPPG-derived heart-rate estimate are reported as quality-flagged computational evidence, never as proof of a clinical condition. The RL module is confined to a finite, reviewed question bank precisely to avoid unconstrained clinical questioning, and the researcher dashboard is explicitly distinguished from a therapist-facing tool. Key limitations include rPPG's sensitivity to video quality and lighting, the difficulty of transferring benchmark performance across differing label definitions [10]–[12], and the ethics-approval requirement for any human-participant study.
VIII. Conclusion and Future Work
This paper presented NOVA, a multimodal embodied research agent that combines cross-modal Transformer fusion of facial, ocular, vocal, linguistic and optional physiological signals with reinforcement-learning-based adaptive question selection and a longitudinal researcher dashboard. Compared against nine existing systems and reviews [1]–[9], NOVA is distinguished by combining full multimodal fusion, learning-driven (rather than scripted) adaptive questioning, and detailed research-output tooling. Future work includes completing the DAIC-WOZ/E-DAIC [10]–[12] benchmark evaluation, validating rPPG feasibility, extending RL safety constraints, and, subject to institutional approval, a small human-participant pilot to assess engagement and reliability.
